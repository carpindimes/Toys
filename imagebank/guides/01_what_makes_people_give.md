# 01 — What Makes People Give: The Psychology Behind the Shot

Our pictures have one job: make someone **stop scrolling, feel something and give**. Research on charitable giving shows clearly what does that. Shoot with these principles in mind every time.

---

## 1. One person beats a crowd

The most robust finding in donation research is the **identifiable victim effect**. People give more to *one identifiable person* than to *many* people, or to a statistic.

- Kogut & Ritov (2005) found that willingness to give to a single identified child was higher than to a group of eight. The effect mostly disappears once there is more than one person in the picture.
- Studies using real donations found that a person identified by **age, name and a picture** raised much more than an unidentified one.
- The reason is emotional, not logical. One face creates empathy and distress, and that drives giving. A crowd or a number makes people think instead of feel, and thinking *lowers* giving.

**On the ground:**
- **Isolate one person.** Even in a crowded aid line, find one face and make it the clear subject. Use the crowd as soft background context.
- **Get close.** Tight framing (head and shoulders, or the face plus their hands) beats wide scenes.
- **Get the name and age** (with consent) so the story can say "Amal, 7" instead of "a child."
- A **pair** works when the relationship is the story: a mother and child, or siblings. Beyond two, the effect weakens fast.

## 2. Emotion on the face is what people feel

Small & Verrochi (2009) found that a **sad facial expression** in a charity image made viewers feel more sympathy and give more than a neutral or happy one. Viewers "catch" the emotion they see.

**On the ground:**
- **The face must be readable.** Use light that shows the eyes, and avoid harsh shadow across the face. Open shade, a doorway or window light, and the golden hour are your friends.
- **Wait for the real moment.** Emotion comes and goes in waves. Stay with your subject and the moment will come. We **never stage or prompt** it, but patience and presence will find it.
- **Eyes and hands carry emotion.** A hand clutching a bowl, a child gripping a parent's sleeve, fingers on a water jug: these details intensify the face.

## 3. Eye contact: use both kinds

The evidence on direct eye contact is mixed. Some studies find direct gaze builds connection; others find an averted, downcast gaze draws more sympathy.

**On the ground:**
- **Shoot both versions** of the same moment when you can: one looking into the lens, and one looking away, down or at a person or object in the scene.
- **Direct gaze** works as a "stop" frame in an ad: it confronts the viewer.
- **An averted gaze** (looking at an empty pot, at the rubble, at a parent) tells a story and creates longing.

## 4. Need + hope = action

Pure despair can make viewers feel helpless and scroll on. Images that show **need alongside something that can be done**, such as a meal, water, a blanket or a smile at receiving them, tell donors *their money works*.

That is why we shoot all three categories:

| Category | What it tells the donor |
|---|---|
| **Dire Need** | "This is urgent. Someone needs you now." |
| **Life Among the Ruins** | "These are people like you, living through the unthinkable." |
| **Receiving Aid** | "Your donation reaches real hands. It works." |

A strong campaign alternates between these. **Every photographer should come back with all three.**

## 5. Dignity sells better *and* is right

Sector codes, such as the **Dóchas Guide to Ethical Communications** used by many European NGOs, ask that people are shown with **dignity, as active people with agency, and with informed consent**. This isn't just ethics. Donors are increasingly put off by "poverty porn", and platforms penalise sensational content.

- Show people **doing**: carrying, cooking, sheltering, comforting, queueing, rebuilding. Don't show them only *suffering*.
- **Never strip someone of dignity** for a shot: no exposed bodies, no moments of humiliation.
- **Consent always.** For children, get consent from a parent or guardian (see guide 03).
