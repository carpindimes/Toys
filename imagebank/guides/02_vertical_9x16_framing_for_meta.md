# 02 — Shooting Vertical (9:16) for Meta Ads

Every shot in the reference bank is **9:16 vertical**, because that's how the ads run: Instagram and Facebook Reels and Stories, full screen on a phone. **Don't shoot landscape and hope we crop it.** Cropping loses resolution and usually cuts the moment in the wrong place.

---

## Technical must-haves

- **Shoot in portrait orientation**, so the camera or phone is upright. If your camera can show a 9:16 crop overlay or frame lines, turn it on. On phones, shoot in the camera's 9:16 / full-screen mode.
- **Minimum 1080 × 1920 pixels**, bigger if you can. Any modern phone or camera exceeds this. Just don't digitally zoom.
- **No text, logos, watermarks, date stamps or filters** baked into the image. We add copy and branding later in the edit. A clean photo is what we need.
- **Keep the horizon level** and the verticals straight, unless a tilt is deliberate.
- **Hold steady and get sharp eyes.** Focus on the nearest eye. A soft face kills the image.

## The safe zones: where NOT to put the face

On Reels and Stories, the app covers parts of the screen with the username and "Sponsored" label at the top, and the caption, buttons and "Donate" call-to-action at the bottom. Third-party guides to Meta's placements agree roughly on:

```
┌────────────────────┐
│   TOP ~14% — UI    │  ← username, "Sponsored". Nothing important here
├────────────────────┤
│                    │
│   SAFE CENTRE      │  ← the FACE and key action go here
│   (eyes ~35–45%    │
│    down the frame) │
│                    │
├────────────────────┤
│ BOTTOM ~20–35% —   │  ← caption, CTA button, icons on the right
│ caption & buttons  │     Keep it simple: ground, texture, hands, context
└────────────────────┘
   ~6% margin each side
```

- **Place the eyes about a third of the way down the frame**, never in the top 14%.
- **Leave the bottom third "quiet"** (ground, rubble texture, the bowl in their hands) so text can sit over it without hiding anything important.
- **Keep the subject away from the right edge**, where the like, comment and share icons sit.

## The first half-second

People decide whether to stop scrolling in well under a second. A frame that stops them has:

1. **A face, big.** It fills a good part of the frame and is readable at thumbnail size.
2. **One clear subject.** No visual clutter competing for the eye.
3. **Contrast.** The subject stands out from the background in light, colour or focus. A bright scarf against grey rubble, or a lit face against a dark tent.
4. **A question.** Something unresolved: what is she looking at? Will he get the food? Where will they sleep?

## Framing patterns that work vertically

| Pattern | How to shoot it |
|---|---|
| **Tight portrait** | Head and shoulders, eyes in the upper third. Fill the frame and crop off the top of the head if needed. |
| **Subject + context** | The person in the bottom half, and the destroyed building rising behind them in the top half. Vertical is perfect for showing the height of the ruins. |
| **Low angle** | Crouch to the child's eye level or lower. This gives them stature and puts the sky or ruins above them. |
| **Hands detail** | Hands holding the aid parcel, bread or water container, with the face soft or out of frame. Great as a second frame. |
| **Foreground frame** | Shoot through a tent opening, a broken window or a gap in the rubble, with the subject inside the frame. |
| **Leading line** | A queue, a path through the rubble or a line of water jugs leading the eye up to the subject. |

## Light

- **Golden hour** (the first and last hour of sun) gives warm, soft light and long shadows. It's ideal for "Life Among the Ruins."
- **Open shade** (beside a wall or under a tarp edge) gives even light on faces, with no squinting.
- **Inside tents**, place the person facing the opening. The doorway becomes a natural softbox.
- **Avoid midday sun on faces.** It causes harsh shadows and squinting. If you must shoot then, turn the person so the sun is behind them and expose for the face.

## Always send a set, not one frame

For every scene, send **3–5 variations**: tight, medium, and wide-but-vertical, plus a hands or detail frame, with both eye contact and averted gaze. The editors need options.
