# 03 — Capturing Real Emotion (Including Children), with Dignity

Our standing policy: **we never stage or prompt emotion.** The moments are real, and the photographer's job is to be ready for them, see them and capture them truthfully and respectfully. This guide is about *how to be ready*.

---

## Be present before you shoot

- **Arrive, put the camera down, talk.** Spend a few minutes with the family before lifting the camera. People relax, the children get bored of you, and natural moments come back.
- **Explain what the photo is for** (raising help for Gaza) and **ask permission.** For anyone under 18, ask the parent or guardian, and ask the child too.
- **Get the name, age and one line of their story** (where they were displaced from, what they need most). A picture with a story raises far more than a picture alone.

## Be ready when the moment comes

- **Pre-set your exposure for the face** so you're not fiddling with settings when it happens.
- **Shoot in short bursts.** Expressions change in a fraction of a second, and the frame between blinks is often the one.
- **Stay through the whole moment, not just the peak.** The second after the tears, when a mother wipes her child's face, is often more powerful than the tears themselves.
- **Watch the reactions, not just the action.** At an aid distribution, the strongest image is often the *face of the person receiving*, not the handover.

## Children: what to look for

Children show emotion openly, and they are the strongest subject for donors. Look for:

- **Waiting**: a child holding an empty pot or bowl in a food queue, looking up toward the distribution point.
- **Carrying**: a small child carrying water jugs, a sibling, or firewood that's too big for them.
- **Protection**: a parent's arms around a child; an older sibling shielding a younger one.
- **Resilience**: children playing in the rubble, flying a kite over the ruins, studying in a tent by daylight. Hope is powerful too.
- **Receiving**: the change in a child's face when they get bread, a blanket or a toy.

**How to shoot them:**
- **Get down to their eye level.** Kneel, crouch or sit. Never shoot down at children from adult height unless you're showing how small they are against the scale of the destruction.
- **Use a slightly longer focal length** (50–85mm equivalent, or the phone's 2× lens) from a respectful distance. You're less intrusive, and you get a flattering perspective with a soft background.
- **Focus on the eyes, every time.**

## The dignity checklist: run it before every shot

- [ ] Has the person (or the child's guardian) agreed?
- [ ] Would they be okay seeing this image on a billboard?
- [ ] Are they clothed and covered as they would want to be?
- [ ] Am I showing them as a *person* (with agency, family, strength), not just a *victim*?
- [ ] Is there **no blood, open wounds, bodies or graphic injury** in the frame? (See guide 04. This also gets ads rejected.)
- [ ] Am I photographing the moment, not creating it?

If any answer is "no," don't take the shot.

## What not to do

- Don't ask anyone to cry, look sad, hold up an empty bowl or "do that again."
- Don't move people into "better" positions to look more desperate. You move; they don't.
- Don't photograph people in moments of humiliation: fighting over food, partially dressed, or in visible distress about being photographed.
- Don't promise anything in exchange for a photo.
